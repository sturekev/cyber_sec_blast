2. NETWORKING PROBLEM / MOTIVATION
================================================================================

THE PROBLEM IN ONE SENTENCE:
Every secure session on today's Internet — TLS, IPsec/IKEv2, SSH, VPN tunnels —
depends on a key-exchange step whose security rests on mathematical problems
(integer factorization, discrete logarithm, elliptic-curve discrete logarithm)
that a sufficiently large fault-tolerant quantum computer is expected to solve
efficiently. If that assumption breaks, the confidentiality layer of the entire
network stack breaks with it.

WHY THIS IS A *NETWORKING* PROBLEM, NOT JUST A MATH PROBLEM:

  (a) HARVEST-NOW / DECRYPT-LATER.
      An adversary with passive tap access to a fiber link, an IXP, or a
      submarine cable can record ciphertext today and store it. The data is
      decrypted years later, once quantum capability arrives. The threat is
      therefore ALREADY ACTIVE for any traffic with a long confidentiality
      lifetime (medical records, financial settlement, national infrastructure
      telemetry, legal archives). Network operators cannot wait for the
      cryptographically relevant quantum computer to appear before responding.

  (b) KEY ESTABLISHMENT IS A PROTOCOL PROBLEM.
      Replacing a cipher is a local change. Replacing a key-exchange is a
      change to the handshake — it alters message sizes, round-trip counts,
      MTU/fragmentation behavior, handshake latency, state machines, and
      middlebox compatibility. These are core CN1 concerns.

  (c) THE REPLACEMENT CANDIDATES HAVE DIFFERENT FAILURE MODES.
      - Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) keeps the existing network model but
        swaps in new hard problems (lattices, hashes, codes). Risk: the new
        math is young and could be broken by classical cryptanalysis.
      - Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) derives keys from measurement
        statistics on a physical quantum channel, so its secrecy argument is
        information-theoretic. Risk: it needs new hardware, new links, trusted
        relay nodes, and it does not scale like IP.
      Neither alone is a drop-in answer. The real engineering question — and
      the question this poster investigates — is how the two are COMBINED and
      where each one attaches to the protocol stack.

MOTIVATING QUESTION DRIVING THE RESEARCH:
  "If an organization deploys QKD hardware, how does a quantum-derived key
   actually reach an application, an IPsec tunnel, or a router line card —
   and what does that cost in latency, throughput, topology, and trust?"

3. BACKGROUND — MINIMUM CONCEPTS A READER NEEDS
================================================================================

B1. SYMMETRIC VS. ASYMMETRIC KEYING
    Bulk encryption (AES-256) is symmetric and is NOT considered broken by
    quantum computing in the same catastrophic way; the practical concern is
    the asymmetric key-AGREEMENT step that bootstraps the symmetric key.
    Conclusion: fix the key agreement, keep the bulk cipher.

B2. QUBITS AND THE PHYSICAL FACTS QKD EXPLOITS
    - A qubit is a two-level quantum system; its state can be a superposition
      of the computational basis states.
    - MEASUREMENT DISTURBS. Measuring in the wrong basis randomizes the
      outcome and leaves a statistical footprint.
    - NO-CLONING THEOREM. An unknown quantum state cannot be copied exactly.
      An eavesdropper therefore cannot make a perfect copy of the transmitted
      photons, keep one, and forward the other untouched.
    These two facts mean that eavesdropping on the quantum channel PRODUCES
    DETECTABLE ERRORS. Secrecy becomes a measurable channel property rather
    than a computational assumption.

B3. QBER (QUANTUM BIT ERROR RATE)
    The fraction of disagreeing bits in the sifted key. QBER is the control
    signal of the whole system: it bounds how much information an eavesdropper
    could hold and therefore determines how much key survives privacy
    amplification. If QBER exceeds the protocol's security threshold, the
    system produces NO key and the session aborts. For BB84 against general
    attacks the commonly cited tolerable threshold is on the order of ~11%
    (one-way reconciliation, asymptotic regime) [17], [21].

B4. THE TWO CHANNELS (critical and often missed)
    QKD always requires BOTH:
      - a QUANTUM channel (dark fiber or free-space/satellite optical path)
        carrying weak optical pulses; and
      - a CLASSICAL, PUBLIC BUT AUTHENTICATED channel carrying the sifting,
        reconciliation, and parameter-estimation messages.
    The classical channel does not need confidentiality, but it absolutely
    needs AUTHENTICATION — otherwise a man-in-the-middle simply impersonates
    both endpoints. This is the single most important architectural
    dependency in the entire technology.

B5. POST-QUANTUM CRYPTOGRAPHY (PQC)
    Classical algorithms believed hard for quantum computers. NIST
    standardized ML-KEM (key encapsulation, FIPS 203), ML-DSA (signatures,
    FIPS 204), and SLH-DSA (stateless hash-based signatures, FIPS 205) in
    August 2024 [7], [8]. PQC runs on ordinary CPUs over ordinary IP — no new
    physical layer required.

B6. "POST-QKD" / HYBRID AS USED IN THIS POSTER
    In this work "post-QKD" denotes the integrated architecture that emerged
    after standalone QKD proved insufficient on its own: QKD supplying
    information-theoretic key material on specific high-value links, PQC
    supplying scalable authentication and a second independent key-agreement
    path, and the final session key derived from BOTH so that the system stays
    secure if EITHER component fails. The attached reference literature
    explicitly identifies hybrid QKD+PQC as the practical near-term path and
    rates the authentication gap as a MEDIUM-severity challenge resolvable in
    the short term (1–3 years) through hybrid solutions [3].

5. EMERGING TECHNOLOGY — WHAT IT IS AND WHERE IT FITS
================================================================================

WHAT IT IS:
QKD is a method for two endpoints to generate a SHARED, SYMMETRIC, UNIFORMLY
RANDOM bit string whose secrecy is guaranteed by the laws of quantum mechanics
plus a security proof, rather than by computational hardness. The canonical
prepare-and-measure protocol is BB84 [1]; the canonical entanglement-based
protocol is E91, which grounds security in violation of Bell's inequality [2].

WHAT IT IS NOT (important for the "not a collection of definitions" standard):
  - It is NOT an encryption algorithm. It produces keys; AES still encrypts.
  - It does NOT provide authentication by itself. It CONSUMES an initial
    authentication secret and must be bootstrapped by something else
    (pre-shared key, PQC signature, or PKI).
  - It is NOT a replacement for the Internet. It is an overlay that feeds
    key material into existing protocols.

WHERE IT FITS IN COMPUTER NETWORKING:
QKD occupies a NEW, PARALLEL PLANE beneath and beside the conventional stack:

   * The quantum channel sits at/below Layer 1 — it is a physical-layer
     facility (dark fiber, DWDM channel, or free-space optical path).
   * The key-management layer (KME) is a control-plane service reachable over
     ordinary IP, exposing a REST interface to applications [4].
   * The consumption point is at Layer 3 (IPsec/IKEv2), Layer 2 (optical/
     Ethernet link encryptors), or Layer 4/7 (TLS, application KMS).

So the correct mental model is: QKD is a KEY SOURCE attached to the control
plane, not a data-carrying network technology.

WHERE PQC FITS:
PQC fits entirely INSIDE existing protocols — ML-KEM in the TLS 1.3 or IKEv2
key exchange, ML-DSA in certificates. It requires no new physical layer, which
is precisely why the hybrid architecture is attractive: PQC gives global reach
and authentication; QKD gives information-theoretic key material on the links
where it is physically available.

6. HOW IT WORKS — MECHANISM AND WORKFLOW
================================================================================

PHASE 0 — BOOTSTRAP AUTHENTICATION (prerequisite)
  Alice and Bob must already share an authentication secret, or must
  authenticate using PQC signatures. Without this, QKD is vulnerable to a
  man-in-the-middle on the classical channel. The reviewed literature
  identifies this authentication dependency as a MEDIUM-severity open
  challenge and points to hybrid QKD+PQC (e.g., lattice-based algorithms) as
  the practical answer, with quantum digital signatures (QDS) as a longer-term
  5–10 year option [3].

PHASE 1 — QUANTUM TRANSMISSION
  Alice encodes random bits onto single-photon-level pulses using randomly
  chosen bases (e.g., rectilinear or diagonal polarization). Bob measures each
  arriving pulse in an independently chosen random basis. Most pulses are lost
  in fiber; only detected events matter.

PHASE 2 — SIFTING
  Over the authenticated CLASSICAL channel, Alice and Bob disclose WHICH BASIS
  they used — never the bit values. Events where bases disagree are discarded.
  Roughly half survive. Result: the SIFTED KEY.
  >> CN1 analogy: a selective-ACK / negotiation pass over a lossy channel.

PHASE 3 — PARAMETER ESTIMATION
  A random subset of the sifted key is publicly compared and then destroyed.
  This measures QBER. QBER is the eavesdropping detector: excess error implies
  either a noisy channel or an interceptor, and the protocol must treat both
  identically (conservatively).

PHASE 4 — ERROR RECONCILIATION
  The remaining bits still mismatch at the QBER rate. Interactive error
  correction (modern implementations use low-leakage LDPC-style codes) makes
  the two strings identical. The parity information exchanged is public, so
  the leakage is accounted for in the next step.

PHASE 5 — PRIVACY AMPLIFICATION
  Alice and Bob apply a shared (randomly selected, publicly agreed) universal
  hash function to compress the reconciled string into a shorter FINAL KEY.
  The compression ratio is chosen so that the eavesdropper's expected
  information about the final key is negligible. This step is what converts
  "partially secret" into "secret."

PHASE 6 — KEY CONFIRMATION & STORAGE
  Both sides verify agreement with an authenticated tag, then write the key
  into the Key Management Entity's pool, indexed by a key_ID.

PHASE 7 — KEY DELIVERY TO APPLICATIONS (the networking step)
  This is where QKD stops being physics and becomes a network service.
  ETSI GS QKD 014 defines a REST-based key delivery API over HTTPS between a
  Secure Application Entity (SAE) and its local Key Management Entity (KME)
  [4]. The model is master-SAE / slave-SAE:

     GET  https://{KME_hostname}/api/v1/keys/{slave_SAE_ID}/status
          -> returns a Status object describing available key material.
     GET/POST .../keys/{slave_SAE_ID}/enc_keys
          -> master SAE obtains keys + key_IDs.
     POST .../keys/{master_SAE_ID}/dec_keys
          -> slave SAE redeems the SAME key by presenting the key_ID.

  The key container is JSON, each entry carrying a UUID-style "key_ID" and a
  base64-encoded "key" value, e.g.:

     { "keys": [
         { "key_ID": "bc490419-7d60-487f-adc1-4ddcc177c139",
           "key":    "wHHVxRwDJs3/bXd38GHP3oe4svTuRpZS0yCC7x4Ly+s=" } ] }

  Error semantics are plain HTTP and map directly to CN1 failure handling:
     400 -> bad request format
     401 -> unauthorized SAE
     503 -> server-side error / key unavailable  (i.e., POOL EXHAUSTION)

  CRITICAL POINT FOR THE POSTER: only the key_ID crosses the public network
  between the two SAEs. The key itself is delivered locally by each KME. The
  classical network transports a POINTER, not a secret.

PHASE 8 — CONSUMPTION IN A SESSION PROTOCOL (the "post-QKD" hybrid step)
  The delivered key is injected as ADDITIONAL ENTROPY into a conventional
  handshake rather than used alone. In IKEv2, the IKE_SA_INIT establishes an
  initial shared secret; CREATE_CHILD_SA exchanges create or rekey child SAs
  and may perform additional key exchanges to add fresh entropy to session
  keys; successive key exchanges are chained through a pseudorandom function
  in a cascade, where PRF+ iterates the PRF to produce a keystream longer than
  a single PRF output block so that the full IKEv2 "keymat"
  { SK_d | SK_ai | SK_ar | SK_ei | SK_er | SK_pi | SK_pr } can be derived
  [6]. Mixing additional secrets into IKEv2 for quantum resistance is
  specified in RFC 8784, and support for multiple sequential key exchanges
  (enabling classical + PQC + QKD-sourced material in one SA) is specified in
  RFC 9370 [9], [10].

  SECURITY PROPERTY OF THE HYBRID: the final session key depends on ALL
  contributing secrets. Breaking elliptic-curve math alone, OR breaking the
  lattice assumption alone, OR compromising the QKD hardware alone, is not
  sufficient. An attacker must defeat every independent component.

PHASE 9 — TRUSTED-NODE RELAY (when the two endpoints are too far apart)
  If A and C have no direct quantum link but both reach node B:
      B holds K_AB (with A) and K_BC (with C).
      B computes and publishes  K_AB XOR K_AC_target  ... operationally:
      B transmits  K_AB XOR K_BC  over the classical channel; C recovers the
      key A intends to share by XOR-ing with its own K_BC.
  The end-to-end key is therefore EXPOSED IN PLAINTEXT INSIDE NODE B.
  B must be physically and operationally trusted. This is the single largest
  architectural weakness of current QKD networks and the reason trusted-node-
  free demonstrations are considered significant milestones [3].

9. REAL-WORLD APPLICATION / EVIDENCE
================================================================================

A. METROPOLITAN AND NATIONAL TESTBEDS (documented in the reviewed literature)
   - SwissQuantum (Geneva, Switzerland), launched 2008, spanning approximately
     20 kilometres — one of the earliest sustained metropolitan QKD testbeds
     [3].
   - Tokyo QKD Network (Japan, from 2010) — a multi-node testbed on NICT's
     JGN-X open fiber network, operated as a collaborative research platform
     across universities and industry and notably involving MULTIPLE VENDORS,
     which makes it an interoperability result and not just a physics result
     [3], [16].
   - United Kingdom (University of Bristol, 2020) — a network built with
     specially developed OPEN-SOURCE software and operated as a TRUSTED-NODE-
     FREE quantum network, directly addressing the trusted-relay weakness
     identified in Section 11 [3].
   - CSIRO Testbed (Australia, 2024) — a laboratory-based QKD research
     environment focused on experimental validation, employing a
     QuintessenceLabs QKD system [3].
   - SECOQC (Vienna, 2008) — early multi-node, multi-technology QKD network
     demonstrating a trusted-repeater network architecture [15].

B. SATELLITE / LONG-HAUL
   - Satellite-to-ground QKD demonstrated with the Micius satellite [12], and
     a subsequently reported integrated space-to-ground quantum communication
     network spanning thousands of kilometres combining fiber backbone and
     satellite links [13]. This is the current answer to the distance limit.

C. PROTOCOL / STANDARDS INTEGRATION (most relevant to CN1)
   - ETSI GS QKD 014 V1.1.1 (Feb 2019) specifies the REST-based key delivery
     API, including the Get status / Get key / Get key with key IDs methods,
     the JSON key container format, and an informative annex on delivering
     keys to MULTIPLE SAEs — i.e., a real, published networking interface,
     not a concept [4].
   - IETF RFC 8784 specifies mixing pre-shared keys into IKEv2 for post-
     quantum security; RFC 9370 specifies multiple key exchanges in IKEv2 —
     together these are the standardized hooks through which QKD-derived and
     PQC-derived secrets enter a production IPsec tunnel [9], [10].
   - Peer-reviewed integration work in IEEE JSAC (2025) demonstrates combining
     classical cryptography, post-quantum cryptography, and QKD inside IKEv2,
     including the CREATE_CHILD_SA / IKE_FOLLOWUP_KE cascade and PRF+ key
     derivation described in Section 6 [6].
   - ITU-T Y.3800-series recommendations define the overview and framework for
     networks supporting QKD [11].

D. CONCRETE DEPLOYMENT SCENARIO TO ILLUSTRATE ON THE POSTER
   A university medical research center and its off-site data center, ~15 km
   apart, connected by leased dark fiber. Patient genomic data must remain
   confidential for decades — a textbook harvest-now/decrypt-later target.
   Design: QKD modules at each end over a dedicated fiber pair; KMEs exposing
   ETSI GS QKD 014; site VPN gateways acting as SAEs; IKEv2 child SAs rekeyed
   every N seconds using hybrid keymat (ECDHE || ML-KEM || QKD key); automatic
   fallback to PQC-only if the key pool returns HTTP 503. Result: the link is
   protected even against an adversary who later breaks both ECC and the
   lattice assumption, and it degrades gracefully instead of failing closed.


================================================================================
10. BENEFITS
================================================================================

B1. INFORMATION-THEORETIC KEY SECRECY (not computational).
    Security of the key does not depend on any unproven hardness assumption.
    Future increases in computing power — quantum or classical — do not
    retroactively compromise keys already generated. WHY IT MATTERS: it is the
    only approach that structurally defeats harvest-now/decrypt-later.

B2. INTRUSION DETECTION BUILT INTO THE PHYSICAL LAYER.
    Eavesdropping raises QBER. The network can therefore DETECT interception
    attempts on the fiber as a measurable, continuous telemetry signal — a
    capability classical optical links do not have. WHY IT MATTERS: it turns
    tapping from an invisible risk into a monitored, alarmable event.

B3. DEFENSE IN DEPTH THROUGH HYBRIDIZATION.
    Because final keys derive from multiple independent secrets, the system
    survives the failure of any single primitive [6], [9], [10]. WHY IT
    MATTERS: it removes the need to bet correctly on which cryptosystem will
    survive — a genuine engineering risk-management win.

B4. CLEAN ARCHITECTURAL SEPARATION AND NON-DISRUPTIVE INTEGRATION.
    A standardized REST key-delivery interface means applications request keys
    without knowing anything about photons [4]. WHY IT MATTERS: vendor
    independence, testability, and the ability to upgrade the quantum layer
    without rewriting applications. The multi-vendor Tokyo testbed is evidence
    this separation works in practice [3], [16].

B5. LONG-LIVED CONFIDENTIALITY FOR HIGH-VALUE, FIXED-ROUTE LINKS.
    Data-center interconnects, inter-branch financial links, national
    infrastructure backhaul, and government circuits are point-to-point and
    long-lived — exactly the topology QKD serves well. WHY IT MATTERS: the
    technology is a poor universal solution but an excellent targeted one, and
    recognizing that is the practical takeaway.

B6. FRESH, HIGH-QUALITY SYMMETRIC KEY SUPPLY ENABLES AGGRESSIVE REKEYING.
    A continuous key source makes frequent rekeying affordable, shrinking the
    volume of data protected by any single key. WHY IT MATTERS: it limits
    blast radius after any future key compromise.

11. LIMITATIONS / CHALLENGES
================================================================================

L1. DISTANCE AND SECRET-KEY RATE (physics-bound).
    Photon loss in fiber is exponential in distance and quantum signals cannot
    be amplified or regenerated (no-cloning). Secret-key rate therefore falls
    sharply with distance, and there is a fundamental rate-distance bound for
    repeaterless links; twin-field QKD was proposed specifically to improve the
    scaling of that limit without quantum repeaters [14]. The reviewed testbed
    survey shows typical research deployments operating over tens of
    kilometres, with national/metro networks reaching a few hundred kilometres
    only by chaining nodes [3].
    >> CN1 FRAMING: this is a throughput-vs-distance constraint, and it forces
       key material to be used for REKEYING rather than for one-time-pad.

L2. THE TRUSTED-NODE PROBLEM (the hardest architectural issue).
    Extending reach requires intermediate nodes that hold end-to-end key
    material in plaintext. Every relay is a new trust anchor and a new attack
    surface; the information-theoretic guarantee applies to each HOP, not to
    the END-TO-END path. Trusted-node-free networks exist in research settings
    [3], but true quantum repeaters are not deployable technology today.

L3. AUTHENTICATION BOOTSTRAP DEPENDENCY.
    QKD requires an authenticated classical channel and therefore cannot be
    the sole root of trust. The reviewed literature classifies this challenge
    as MEDIUM severity, resolvable in the SHORT TERM (1–3 years) via hybrid
    QKD+PQC (e.g., lattice-based algorithms), with full reliance on quantum-
    native authentication such as quantum digital signatures estimated at
    5–10 years [3].

L4. DEDICATED PHYSICAL INFRASTRUCTURE AND COST.
    QKD generally needs dark fiber or a dedicated wavelength (Raman noise from
    co-propagating classical channels degrades quantum signal quality),
    specialized sources and single-photon detectors, and in some designs
    cryogenic cooling. Capital and operating costs are high relative to a
    software PQC upgrade, and they scale per-link, not per-user.

L5. NO ROUTING, NO SWITCHING, NO MULTICAST IN THE QUANTUM PLANE.
    Quantum states cannot be stored in a buffer, copied to multiple ports, or
    forwarded by a conventional router. Every fundamental mechanism that makes
    IP networks scalable is unavailable. QKD topologies are therefore point-
    to-point or optically switched — fundamentally unlike the Internet.

L6. IMPLEMENTATION / SIDE-CHANNEL ATTACKS.
    Security proofs assume idealized devices. Real equipment has deviated from
    the model, enabling attacks against detectors and sources; the field's
    response includes measurement-device-independent protocols and extensive
    analysis of practical device security [21]. The theory being
    unconditionally secure does NOT make the product unconditionally secure.

L7. AUTHORITATIVE SKEPTICISM FROM SECURITY AGENCIES.
    The U.S. NSA has stated it does not support the use of QKD to protect
    communications in National Security Systems and does not anticipate
    certifying it, citing the need for special-purpose equipment, the trusted-
    relay/insider risk, immature validation, and the lack of authentication
    [18]. The UK NCSC has likewise advised that PQC, not QKD, is the primary
    mitigation for the quantum threat in most settings [19].
    >> THIS BELONGS ON THE POSTER. The critical-thinking rubric rewards
       presenting evidence that cuts against the technology, not only for it.

L8. KEY POOL EXHAUSTION AND AVAILABILITY.
    If the key generation rate falls below demand — due to fiber degradation,
    temperature, construction work, or simple over-subscription — the KME
    returns an error (HTTP 503) and dependent tunnels lose their quantum key
    source [4]. A deployment without a defined PQC fallback converts a
    confidentiality technology into an AVAILABILITY risk.

L9. INTEROPERABILITY AND STANDARDS IMMATURITY.
    Although ETSI GS QKD 014/004 and ITU-T Y.3800 exist [4], [5], [11],
    vendor implementations and physical-layer parameters still differ widely,
    and multi-vendor operation remains a research achievement rather than a
    routine procurement option [3], [16].

L10. ENERGY AND OPERATIONAL OVERHEAD.
    Detectors, stabilization, and (for some systems) cooling consume power
    continuously, regardless of how much key is actually consumed — a poor
    efficiency profile for lightly used links.

JUSTIFICATION NARRATIVE:
The technology clears the "experimental only" bar because (i) a published,
implementable network API exists and is being used across vendors [4], (ii)
national testbeds have operated for over a decade with documented maturity
levels ranging from low to high [3], and (iii) peer-reviewed work now
demonstrates integration into a production-grade protocol (IKEv2) rather than
into a laboratory one-off [6]. It does NOT clear the "deployed" bar because
reach still depends on trusted relays, cost confines it to high-value links,
multi-vendor interoperability is not routine, and influential national security
authorities currently recommend PQC over QKD for general use [18], [19].

MOST ACCURATE ONE-LINE CLASSIFICATION FOR THE POSTER:
"QKD is an EMERGING technology in active STANDARDIZATION with niche
 DEPLOYMENT; PQC is STANDARDIZED and deploying now; the HYBRID of the two is
 where current networking research and early engineering effort is
 concentrated."

13. FUTURE IMPACT (5–15 YEARS)
================================================================================

NEAR TERM (1–3 YEARS) — PQC becomes the default; QKD becomes a premium option.
  Hybrid key exchange (classical + ML-KEM) becomes standard in TLS 1.3 and
  IKEv2 deployments. QKD appears as an optional additional entropy source on
  selected high-value links. The reviewed literature places hybrid QKD+PQC
  authentication solutions squarely in this 1–3 year window, and rates key
  extraction efficiency as essentially solved within 1–2 years by modern
  low-leakage reconciliation codes [3].

MID TERM (3–7 YEARS) — the key becomes a managed network resource.
  Expect "Key-as-a-Service" / QKD-as-a-Service offerings in which carriers
  expose ETSI-compliant key APIs to tenants. Key pools will be monitored,
  budgeted, and SLA'd exactly like bandwidth, with telemetry for key rate,
  QBER, and pool depth feeding standard NOC dashboards. Crypto-agility —
  the ability to switch key sources at runtime without re-architecting —
  becomes a procurement requirement. QBER monitoring may be marketed as a
  physical-layer intrusion detection feature in its own right.

LONGER TERM (7–15 YEARS) — conditional on quantum repeaters.
  IF entanglement swapping with practical quantum memory matures, the trusted-
  node requirement disappears and genuine END-TO-END information-theoretic
  keys over long distances become possible. That would change the trust model
  of the Internet more than any change since the introduction of public-key
  cryptography. Combined with satellite links already demonstrated at
  continental scale [13], the plausible outcome is a hybrid global fabric:
  PQC everywhere by default, QKD on backbone and high-assurance segments,
  satellites bridging continents, with routing decisions that account for
  trust boundaries as well as cost and latency. Realistically, repeaters
  remain the gating uncertainty; if they do not mature, QKD stays a valuable
  but bounded point-to-point technology rather than a networking paradigm.

WHAT STUDENTS/ENGINEERS SHOULD TAKE AWAY ABOUT CAREER-RELEVANT CHANGE:
  Network engineers will increasingly manage CRYPTOGRAPHIC INVENTORY the way
  they manage IP address space today: knowing which algorithm protects which
  flow, how long its material lives, and how to rotate it under pressure.


================================================================================
14. CONCLUSION
================================================================================

MOST IMPORTANT FINDING:
The decisive problem in quantum-era networking is not generating quantum keys —
that physics has been demonstrated repeatedly since 1984 and runs in
metropolitan testbeds today. The decisive problem is DELIVERING and CONSUMING
those keys inside real protocol stacks under real constraints of distance,
rate, trust, and availability. Standardized interfaces such as the ETSI
REST-based key delivery API and standardized IKEv2 extensions for multiple key
exchanges are what actually convert QKD from a laboratory result into a
network service.

SECOND, EQUALLY IMPORTANT FINDING:
QKD does not eliminate trust; it RELOCATES it — from mathematical assumptions
to physical devices, fiber plant, and trusted relay facilities. Any honest
assessment must say so, which is why national security agencies currently
favor PQC for general use while research continues on trusted-node-free
architectures.

THE PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY:
The realistic architecture of the next decade is HYBRID and defense-in-depth:
PQC everywhere for reach and authentication, QKD where physics and budget
permit, and a key schedule that combines both so that no single broken
assumption compromises the session. For a CN1 student, the enduring lesson is
that a security technology succeeds or fails on ordinary networking
properties — latency, throughput, queuing, topology, availability, and
interoperability — not on the elegance of its underlying theory.

APPENDIX A — SUGGESTED POSTER ABSTRACT (for the required .docx)
================================================================================
Word count: ~215. Adjust to your instructor's limit.

Modern network security depends on key-exchange mechanisms whose secrecy rests
on computational hardness assumptions that quantum computing is expected to
invalidate, creating an immediate "harvest-now, decrypt-later" risk for traffic
with long confidentiality lifetimes. This project examines Quantum Key
Distribution (QKD) and its integration with Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) as
a networking problem rather than a physics problem. QKD derives symmetric keys
from measurement statistics on a quantum channel, so eavesdropping produces a
measurable quantum bit error rate rather than silent compromise. However, QKD
requires a separate authenticated classical channel, cannot be routed,
amplified, or multicast, and extends its reach only through trusted relay
nodes that hold key material in plaintext. The work maps QKD across the
protocol stack: generation at Layer 1, orchestration through the ETSI
GS QKD 014 REST key-delivery API at Layer 7, and consumption at Layers 2–4,
where IKEv2 multiple key-exchange extensions (RFC 8784, RFC 9370) allow
classical, post-quantum, and quantum-derived secrets to be cascaded into a
single session key. CN1 concepts of throughput, queuing, delay, error control,
and availability are applied to key-rate limits and key-pool exhaustion.
Evidence from metropolitan and national testbeds supports classifying QKD as
emerging with active standardization, while the practical near-term
architecture is hybrid: PQC for reach and authentication, QKD for high-value
links, and defense in depth across both.
